\begin{abstract}
The software P4 switch bmv2 anchors much of the P4 research pipeline, and papers
routinely report its forwarding performance in Mbps. We surveyed 18 papers that
measure or relay bmv2 throughput: reported figures span ${\sim}2{,}500\times$
(0.57\,Mbps to 1.4\,Gbps), none reports build flags or provides enough
information to reconstruct the binary, and two peer-reviewed papers order bmv2
versus Open vSwitch in opposite directions. We then quantified, under
preregistered protocols on one machine, three variables that no surveyed paper
isolates. (1)~\emph{Build:} the officially recommended compile configuration
versus a default-style build is $12\times$ on a three-hop production path and
$R=8.0$ (quantisation interval 5.14--12.0) isolated to a single hop with the
control plane live --- so part of the production-path ratio is not the compiler
flags. (2)~\emph{Unit:} across 64/256/1024\,B frames, clean-rate pps varies
$1.25\times$ while the same data span $16.0\times$ in Mbit/s --- Mbps without a
frame size is a $16\times$ ambiguity. (3)~\emph{Flows:} per-flow highest clean
rate falls monotonically with flow count, replicated, while OvS at matched
working points does not. In two of our three experiments, our own instrument's
limit nearly became the reported number; none of the 18 papers states what
limited its measurement.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{P4, BMv2, software switches, benchmarking, reproducibility, network emulation}

\section{A 2,500$\times$ spread nobody can arbitrate}

Of 18 surveyed papers that measure or relay bmv2 performance, 12 measure it
directly. Among those twelve: \textbf{0/12} report compiler flags or
optimisation level, \textbf{0/12} run any build comparison, 3/12 name the
variant (all from one lab's lineage), 1/12 names a version, and one gives a
single qualitative sentence about compiling without logging. Their throughput
figures span ${\sim}2{,}500\times$, from 0.57\,Mbps at 64\,B
\cite{hasnaa2023tssa} to 1.4\,Gbps on physical NICs \cite{kumazoe2023icncc}.
With the information the papers themselves provide, no two of these numbers can
be shown to measure the same artifact. We say \emph{mutually incomparable}
deliberately --- we did not attempt reproductions, so we claim nothing about
reproducibility.

The consequence is concrete: one line of work measures Open vSwitch two orders
of magnitude above bmv2 \cite{chen2025tomacs,chen2023pads}, while another
concludes the P4 pipeline outperforms OvS \cite{fernando2025network} (a
reactive-control-plane effect, per its own analysis) --- and a reader cannot
arbitrate, because neither reports its build. Even within one lab's lineage,
adjacent papers differ $1.7\times$ in per-switch RTT with no build information
on either side \cite{waind2024pads,waind2026pads}.

The missing variable is not obscure. The bmv2 repository's README warns that
build flags ``can have a massive impact on performance''
\cite{bmv2repo}, and its performance documentation lists the recommended
configuration; two of the surveyed papers cite that very document and still do
not report their own build. The knowledge exists in project documentation and
forums; the \emph{reporting norm} does not exist in the 18 papers. The adjacent
NFV benchmarking community shows the norm is attainable: its flagship study
pins per-switch commits, build notes, and parameters
\cite{zhang2021benchmarking}. Our study is a P4-community instance of a
familiar genre: an unreported, ``trivial'' variable large enough to invalidate
published comparisons \cite{mytkowicz2009wrong}.

\section{Three preregistered measurements}

All experiments preregistered outcome intervals \emph{and the meaning of every
outcome} before data; the replication unit is the interleaved arm (fresh fabric
per arm); ladders are $\times1.5$ ($\pm$20\% per rung); the running binary is
identified by symbol signature against a negative control, never by PATH; CPU
contamination gates use per-process attribution and were trusted only after a
positive control made them fire. Both endpoint counters and paired
ingress-RX/egress-TX interface counters are read in every arm --- kernel drop
counters read zero even in arms where bmv2 lost 79.66\% of packets internally.
Raw data is archived by content hash.

\textbf{(1) Build.} \emph{Stock} is a default-style build (\texttt{-O0}, logging
compiled in, as installed by common guides); \emph{fast} is the officially
recommended composite configuration (\texttt{-O3 --disable-logging-macros
--disable-elogger}). On our three-hop production path the UDP zero-loss point
differs $12\times$. Isolated to a single switch --- one hop, control plane
still live --- it is $45$ vs.\ $360$ Mbit/s: $R=8.0$, zero spread across arms,
quantisation interval $(5.14,12.0)$, knee-shape interpolation ${\approx}7.8$.
This landed in the preregistered outcome ``part of the production-path ratio is
not the compiler flags.'' We report \emph{both} working points; whether the
difference is path length or the live control plane is preregistered future
work, and this abstract attributes it to neither.

\textbf{(2) Unit.} Sweeping the standard NFV frame-size axis
\cite{zhang2021benchmarking}, clean-rate pps is near-constant while the same
cells span $16.0\times$ as bit rate (Table~\ref{tab:results}); the registered
ratios $P(256)/P(64){=}1.25$ and $P(1024)/P(64){=}1.00$ fall in the
pps-ceiling interval and far from the bps-ceiling prediction (0.15--0.40 /
0.03--0.12). A registered sender-side control passed with $9.6\times$ headroom,
so the flat line is bmv2's, not the generator's; all six arms also truncated at
the same pps rung under a rule that is blind to frame size. \emph{Reporting
bmv2 capacity in Mbps without the frame size is therefore not imprecision; it
is a $16\times$ ambiguity.} All 18 surveyed papers report Mbps (one adds a
single pps point \cite{kohler2018p4cep}); none sweeps frame size for bmv2 ---
one paper's own two sizes imply near-constant pps, unanalysed in its text
\cite{hasnaa2023tssa}.

\textbf{(3) Flows.} With all $n$ flows pinned to one path class (so
flows/switch $=$ flows/link $=n$ by construction), the per-flow highest clean
rate falls monotonically in each of two independent arms, cells agreeing
within one ladder rung. The aggregate is an arithmetic consequence; we report
no ratio --- an earlier ``collapse factor'' divided a clean threshold by a
saturation knee, two different quantities, and was withdrawn. OvS at matched
working points showed no decline (earlier measurement; a same-ladder control
is planned).

\section{What limited our measurements}

Each experiment's dominant threat turned out to be the same: \emph{our own
instrument's ceiling masquerading as the system's}. It happened twice --- a
flow-count ladder's top rung was mistaken for the switch's single-flow ceiling
long enough to enter a draft, and the first build-ratio round's ladder ended
below the fast build's ability, censoring the numerator --- and was blocked
once, by the registered sender gate. Both slips were caught by rule-driven
ladder tops and post-hoc checks, and both corrections moved \emph{away} from
the more publishable answer. None of the 18 surveyed papers states what
limited its measurement; we read this as the same disease as Mbps-without-size,
in a second form. Two further disciplines earned their keep: preregistering
the meaning of unwelcome outcomes (the build ratio narrowing to $8.0\times$
is a result, not a failure), and recognising that replication cannot narrow
quantisation --- ten arms on the same rung say nothing about where inside the
rung the true value sits.

\section{Takeaway}

We propose a four-line provenance minimum for any software-switch number:
\textbf{build} (flags and commit, or exact package), \textbf{unit} (pps with
frame size, or Mbps with the size stated), \textbf{placement} (flows per
switch and per link), and \textbf{limit} (what stopped the measurement ---
the system, or the instrument). Preregistrations, raw data (content-hashed),
and scripts will be released.
